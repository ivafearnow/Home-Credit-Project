Q1. How strongly does any single applicant characteristic separate borrowers who struggle to repay from those who do not — and is any one of them strong enough to stand alone?

Traces to: “identify borrower characteristics that consistently indicate repayment difficulty” and “compare a transparent baseline with predictive alternatives.”

Why this is first: everything downstream depends on knowing whether this problem has a few strong signals or many weak ones. It also determines what “transparent baseline” means in practice — if one variable carries most of the separation, that variable is the baseline, and the modeling stage has to beat it rather than beat coin-flipping.

Method: default rate by decile for every numeric column; ratio of worst-decile to best-decile default rate as a separation measure. Point-biserial correlation as a secondary sort, not the headline — an 8% base rate keeps all correlations small and the number understates real separation.

Output: a ranked table of the top ~15 variables with their decile spread, and decile plots for the top 3.

Decision it forces: which variables go forward, and whether the answer to my BPS is “a few strong predictors” or “many weak ones that only work in combination.” These imply different recommendations.

Q2. Is missingness a defect to be repaired, or a signal in its own right?

Traces to: “variables will be assessed for predictive value, availability, and ease of verification.” Builds on standing check 2.

Why here: this has to resolve before Q3, because a column that is 56% missing but whose missingness predicts default is a different kind of asset than one that is simply incomplete — and it gets screened differently.

Method: for each column above ~20% missing, compare default rate for missing vs. present. Then check whether missingness clusters — are the building-characteristic columns missing on the same rows?

Output: a table of missing-vs-present default rates, and a co-missingness heatmap for the high-null block.

Decision it forces: impute, add a missing-indicator, or drop — per column, with a stated reason. Goes in decisions.md.

What I expect and should be suspicious of: the EXT_SOURCE_1 gap looked small in the standing checks (8.52% missing vs 7.50% present). Small differences on 307K rows are statistically significant and practically negligible. I need to say which one this is rather than reporting the p-value.

Q3. Which predictors survive a screen on all three of predictive value, availability, and verifiability — not just the first?

Traces to: directly to my BPS’s “focused set of reliable, readily available variables” and to the deliverable being “an executive recommendation on risk factors… not simply the highest-scoring model.”

Why this is the question that most distinguishes my project: a pure Kaggle approach optimizes predictive value alone. My BPS commits to two additional criteria, which means a strong predictor can still fail. The obvious tension: the EXT_SOURCE variables look like the strongest signals, but they are opaque third-party scores — an underwriter cannot verify one, and an applicant cannot be told why theirs is low. EXT_SOURCE_1 is also over half missing.

Method: classify each candidate from Q1 on three axes — separation strength (Q1), completeness (standing check 2), and verification burden (self-reported / document-backed / externally sourced, assigned by me from the data dictionary). Present as a table or a quadrant plot.

Output: a shortlist of variables that pass all three, and an explicit list of strong predictors I am setting aside because of availability or verifiability.

Decision it forces: the second list is the more interesting one and should be defended in the results section. This is where EDA shapes the recommendation rather than just describing data.

Q4. Do the money variables behave the way an underwriter would expect?

Traces to: “borrower characteristics that consistently indicate repayment difficulty”; also the “what would an underwriter refuse to believe?” test.

Why: income, credit amount, annuity, and goods price are the variables a credit committee will ask about first, regardless of what the correlation table says. If higher income does not monotonically reduce default risk, that is a finding worth the space. Ratios may also carry more signal than levels — credit-to-income and annuity-to-income are what an underwriter actually reasons about, and neither exists as a column yet.

Method: default rate by income decile, credit decile, and by two derived ratios (AMT_CREDIT / AMT_INCOME_TOTAL, AMT_ANNUITY / AMT_INCOME_TOTAL). Handle the 117M income outlier identified in standing check 3 before plotting — state the treatment rather than silently winsorizing.

Output: four decile plots plus a short table comparing the separation of the raw variables against the derived ratios.

Decision it forces: whether to engineer the ratio features for the modeling stage, and how to treat the income outliers.

Q5. How much of the 122 columns is actually redundant?

Traces to: “a focused set” — I cannot recommend a focused set without knowing what is duplicative.

Why: the building-characteristic block appears three times over (_AVG, _MODE, _MEDI), and AMT_CREDIT and AMT_GOODS_PRICE are near-identical by construction. If 122 columns are really ~40 distinct constructs, the recommendation gets much simpler and the multicollinearity risk for a transparent (logistic) baseline gets much smaller.

Method: correlation matrix on numeric columns, clustered; report groups above |r| > 0.9 and one representative per group.

Output: a clustered correlation heatmap and a table of redundancy groups with a chosen representative for each.

Decision it forces: which representative to keep per group, and whether the count of genuinely independent constructs changes how I frame the recommendation.

Q6. Does the repayment-difficulty rate vary enough across major customer segments to threaten the stability my BPS promises?

Traces to: “results must also be checked for stability across major customer segments,” and to the broader cross-market concern — that predictors validated on one population may not transfer.

Why: this is the only question that speaks to my BPS’s central worry. I cannot test cross-market transfer with a single-market dataset, but within-dataset heterogeneity is the best available proxy: if the default rate already swings widely across education level or income type inside one population, that is evidence for the caution my BPS recommends, not against it.

Method: default rate with confidence intervals by CODE_GENDER, NAME_EDUCATION_TYPE, NAME_INCOME_TYPE, NAME_FAMILY_STATUS, NAME_HOUSING_TYPE, REGION_RATING_CLIENT, and OCCUPATION_TYPE. Report segment sizes alongside rates — a 40% default rate on 180 rows is not a finding.

Output: ordered bar charts with counts annotated, and a table of the widest-spread segments.

Decision it forces: which segments the modeling stage must report performance separately for. Also flags any segment where using the variable at all raises a fair-lending concern — worth naming, since my BPS mentions responsible-lending oversight.

Q7. What does the distribution of the strongest predictors imply about where a decision threshold could plausibly sit? (candidate for cutting)

Traces to: the success metric — “at least a 5% relative reduction in the repayment-difficulty rate at the same simulated approval rate.”

Why I am tempted: it shows the shape of the approval/difficulty trade-off before any model exists, which makes the modeling stage’s target concrete instead of abstract.

Why I might cut it: it requires inventing a composite score (e.g., the mean of the three EXT_SOURCE columns), and a hand-built composite is a model — a bad one, fit on the same data I would evaluate it on. That is arguably out of scope for EDA and gives a falsely optimistic curve.

Method if kept: rank applicants by the composite, then plot cumulative approval rate against realized default rate among the approved. Label it clearly as illustrative, not a result.

Decision: keep as an explicitly-labeled illustration, or defer to the modeling stage. Leaning toward deferring, and instead closing the notebook by stating what the threshold analysis will need.
